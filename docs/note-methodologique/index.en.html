Methodology note for discussion with healthcare professionals

How OUCH calculates its figures

Formulas, thresholds, sources and limits of each indicator in the report, so that you can check and challenge them.

Reference document
29 October 2026
OUCH v0.7.1

What this note describes

OUCH is a pain diary that the person fills in themselves, in under a minute a day. Its report summarises that diary with a few calculations: means, breakdown of days, comparison with the previous period, flares, before-and-after comparisons around a treatment change, correlations. For each one, this note gives the formula, the threshold, its source and its limit.

How to read it

The data is self-reported and nothing is interpreted: no diagnosis, no treatment advice. Conditions, “diagnosed” or “suspected”, are declared by the patient and reproduced as they are. References, numbered in square brackets as in the report, are listed at the end of the note. When a choice is specific to OUCH and not consensual, such as the definition of flares, the note says so.

Principles that apply to every calculation

  • Use only the days with an entry. A missing day is excluded, never estimated or filled in.
  • State, with each figure, the number of days it rests on.
  • Cite the source of each threshold and say when it is extrapolated.

What the report does not do

  • Make a diagnosis or advise a treatment.
  • Correct correlations for autocorrelation or multiple comparisons, or compute a p-value.
  • Present an association as a cause.
  • Send the data anywhere: everything is computed on the person’s device.

2 · Periods and completeness

NotionDefinition
Analysed periodFrom the date of the last appointment (entered at export time) to the report date, both included: N = (report date − appointment) + 1 days.
Previous periodThe N days before the appointment, hence the same length. All comparisons in the report are made against this baseline.

4 · Flare detection

A flare is a run of days clearly above what is usual for the person, not above a fixed value: 7/10 is an ordinary day for one person and an emergency for another. There is no consensus definition of a flare; this one is specific to OUCH. It reuses the minimal clinically important difference[2][4], established for improvement and applied here to a worsening, which is an extrapolation. It is not looked for in someone who follows only migraine: a migraine comes in attacks, with pain-free days between, and reading “above the usual” would say nothing true; the report then counts headache days instead (§ 11). It is computed over the whole history, because a day’s usual level needs entries from before the report period. An episode is attributed to the period in which it starts; its days are counted where they fall.

ParameterValueRole
Usual levelMedian pain of the previous 28 days, excluding days already in a flareThe median is little affected by extreme days. At least 10 days with an entry are needed in this window; otherwise nothing is detected and the report says “too few days with an entry”.
Flare thresholdusual + max(2 points; 30% × usual)Above a usual level of about 7.7/10, the threshold exceeds 10: the person cannot be detected this way, which is better than an invented threshold.
Floor4/10To open an episode, pain must also reach 4.
Minimum length3 consecutive days above the thresholdA day without an entry between two high days does not break the run. A shorter episode is discarded.
Return to usualpain ≤ usual + 1A day in between resets the count of return days.
End of an episode2 days with an entry back at the usual levelA gap of more than 3 days between two entries closes the episode with an “unknown” end. The last day of an episode is its last day above the threshold.
Ongoing episodelast high day at most 1 day before the report date, with no returnFlagged as such in the report.
Worked example. Fourteen days of pain between 3 and 4: the usual level is the median, 3, and the threshold 3 + max(2; 0.9) = 5. The next five days are 6, 7, 5, 8 and 6, all ≥ 5: a 5-day episode, peak 8/10. Then a 4 (≤ 3 + 1: back to usual) and a 3: two return days, the episode is closed, with a return in 1 day. This case is in the software’s tests.

What comes before a flare (detailed version)

For each flare, the report gives the mean of the 3 previous days for six measures (sleep duration and quality, stress, fatigue, brain fog, mood, with at least 2 days with an entry), and below it the usual mean of the 28 days before, excluding flares (at least 10 days). Treatment changes noted in the previous 14 days are added. Nothing is highlighted: in a trial where anything more than one standard deviation away was flagged, something was flagged before 37% of the 3-day windows that precede no flare, and the reader would see a cause in it.

5 · Treatments

IndicatorCalculation
TypesRegular: taken every day, ticked by default, the person unticks a missed dose. As needed: number of doses noted, with a prescribed daily maximum. Unspecified: treatment added without a description. Each dose change closes a dosage period and opens another.
Days takenDays with at least one dose (number of doses absent or > 0).
Self-reported adherence (regular)days taken / (days taken + days explicitly marked missed). Days without an entry are left out of the calculation.
Doses (as needed)Sum of doses; maximum in one day, flagged if it exceeds the prescribed maximum. Side effects: each effect reported, with number of days and first and last dates.
Relief (as needed)Count of doses by level; in the short version, the share of rated doses judged “moderate or major”: (moderate + major) / rated doses. Not asked for regular treatments.
Rescue-medication daysDays with at least one dose of an “as needed” or “unspecified” treatment, out of the days with an entry. Same calculation within each flare.

Limit specific to these calculations: since a regular treatment is ticked by default, a forgotten dose that was not unticked counts as taken, so self-reported adherence may be overestimated.

6 · Factors associated with pain (detailed version)

Spearman correlation (ρ) between a factor and the pain of the same day, over both periods combined: it is the Pearson correlation computed on ranks, ties receiving their mean rank. It assumes neither a straight line nor a normal distribution and suits 0–10 scales. A factor is shown only from 14 pairs of values, and not if either of the two is constant.

Example. Sleep (quality) 4, 6, 6, 7, 8, 5, 3, 7 and pain 7, 5, 6, 4, 3, 5, 8, 5 over eight days. Sleep ranks: 2, 4.5, 4.5, 6.5, 8, 3, 1, 6.5; pain ranks: 7, 4, 6, 2, 1, 4, 8, 4. The correlation of these two lists of ranks gives ρ = −0.89: better nights go with lower pain, over these eight days. Dotted line: a visual guide only.
FactorValue paired with the day’s pain
Sleep (quality, duration)Value entered that day, which describes the previous night. The link runs both ways, more often sleep → pain[14].
Stress, fatigueValue of the day. Fatigue is a co-symptom, not a factor.
Previous day’s activityActivity of the previous calendar day (an entry is needed that day). Too much and too little activity are both associated with more pain[15]: a monotonic correlation can therefore miss it.
Positive actionsNumber of actions noted that day. The link may run both ways.
Atmospheric pressureChange in hPa since the last day with an entry. Inconsistent literature[7][11][12][13].

Strength. |ρ| < 0.1: negligible; < 0.3: weak; < 0.5: moderate; above: strong (conventional labels, with no test, p-value or confidence interval). Median comparisons (at least 3 days per group): the day after a very active day (previous day’s activity ≥ 8); at least 2 positive actions versus none; period days versus the others, if the person includes them.

7 · Before and after a change of regular treatment

New in version 0.3.0 (the other calculations are unchanged since 0.2.1, apart from the day-of-week table, added in 0.4.0, section 3). For each start, dose change or stop of a regular treatment, the report (both versions, for the period and the 5 weeks before it) and the Trends screen compare the diary before and after. It is a description of two periods: it does not say whether the treatment “works”. As-needed treatments have no comparison: what they do is itself a measure.

ParameterDefinition
Periods comparedDate of the change: start or new dose, its first day; stop, the day after the last day taken. Before: the 28 days before. Settling in: the following 7 days, not counted (a regular treatment rarely acts on the first day). After: the following 28 days. 14 days with an entry are needed on each side, otherwise no figure; their number (before · after) is always shown. Until “after” has elapsed, the comparison is “in progress”.
MeasuresMean pain; flare days out of 28; as-needed doses per day with an entry (a dose without a number counts as one). Flagged under “Good to know”, never corrected: another regular-treatment change in the period, or a change made during a flare.
Reading the comparison. The settling-in week is set aside. At the bottom, mean pain before and after is placed next to the patient’s usual variation. Fictitious figures: here the drop falls outside the band, which only says it is not ordinary for them, not that the treatment works.

The patient’s usual variation

The diary is cut into 28-day periods from the first day with an entry (at least 14 days with an entry in each, excluding those that overlap the comparison). The usual variation is the 80th percentile (type 7) of the absolute differences between consecutive periods, with at least 6 pairs. The report says “8 differences out of 10 between two consecutive periods are below X points”.

Limits. One comparison per change, with no test. An improvement may follow a treatment started at the bottom of a painful period (regression to the mean), the weather, the season or another change.

8 · Where the method is solid, where it is less so

1. Solid

Standard statistics, formulas published here and tested: 415 automated tests pass on this version, before-and-after comparisons included.

Validated 0–10 numeric scale, 24-hour recall, missing days never imputed.

2. More fragile

Flare definition, extrapolated and non-consensual. Before-and-after comparisons: no test, exposed to regression to the mean. Mild, moderate, severe categories are conventional. Fatigue, sleep and mood scales (and § 11’s) not validated.

Seven correlations read without correction for multiple comparisons, on non-independent days.

3. Missing

No clinical validation of OUCH itself: no comparison with a validated instrument (BPI, for example), and no flares confirmed by patients or clinicians.

Missing days are not necessarily random: people sometimes fill in less on bad days. Nothing is verified by a clinician.

9 · What the patient noticed (report)

In both versions of the report, a section carries the leads the patient chose to keep for the appointment (appendix, “Trends: leads”), recomputed over the two periods of the report with the same rule. Each line gives the mean pain and the number of days of the two groups compared. One-week trials that ended in these periods are added, when each kind of day counts at least 2; the report reminds that seven days is little. A kept lead that no longer passes the rule on the report’s data is left out. Nothing in it is interpreted: these are the patient’s observations, unverified, with no cause established. Limits: the Trends leads are read without correction for multiple comparisons, on non-independent days, and the patient keeps only the ones that speak to them: it is a selection. The actions in trials are the ones they noted themselves (free text); OUCH suggests none.

Ten questions for you

  1. Does the definition of flares (at least 3 days more than 2 points or 30% above the 28-day median) make sense to you? Which criterion would you prefer?
  2. Are the categories ≤ 3, 4–6, ≥ 7 useful to you, or do you read the person’s course against their own baseline?
  3. Are a 7-day mean and a period compared with the previous one enough, or are other summaries needed?
  4. Are correlations useful in consultation, or are they at risk of being over-interpreted? Should they be removed from the general practitioner version?
  5. Which indicators do you really use, and which would you remove from the report?
  6. What data do you lack today to make decisions, that a diary could provide?
  7. Is the before-and-after comparison around a treatment change (28 days before, 7 days not counted, 28 days after, with the patient’s usual variation) a useful format? What length would you choose?
  8. Is the “What the patient noticed” section (kept leads, one-week trials) useful to you, or might it steer the appointment towards a cause nothing establishes? Should it be removed from the general practitioner version?
  9. Is the split into 25 areas (the 19 WPI regions with the side, plus head, hands, feet and pelvis) useful to you? Should there be an intensity per area, or another split?
  10. Do the labels, bands and illness profiles of the indicators in § 11 seem right to you? Which sensitive points (bowel, urinary, fever) should be changed or removed?

11 · Other indicators noted (report and Trends)

These indicators are optional and in early access, until clinicians have reviewed them. They are simple scales or answers, not validated, and no questionnaire name appears in the app. The report and the Trends card “What else you noted” describe them with the same sentences; nothing is interpreted or compared with a norm. A day left blank is never counted as “no”: each share is worked out over the days on which the question was answered (“no” and “none” are answers).

References, and what each one contributes

Each summary restates the main conclusion of the study, from its official abstract (PubMed); it is the same text as in the app’s “Scientific sources” section. These studies concern groups of patients: they establish nothing certain for a given person.

  1. Dworkin RH, Turk DC, Farrar JT, et al. Core outcome measures for chronic pain clinical trials: IMMPACT recommendations. Pain. 2005;113(1-2):9-19. PMID 15621359.
    What the study says. Expert consensus (IMMPACT) on the measures to include in clinical trials of chronic pain: pain intensity, rated on a 0 to 10 numeric scale, is one of them, along with functioning, mood and the global impression of improvement. In OUCH. Pain is rated each day from 0 to 10, as in clinical trials, which makes the figures readable by a clinician.
  2. Farrar JT, Young JP Jr, LaMoreaux L, Werth JL, Poole MR. Clinical importance of changes in chronic pain intensity measured on an 11-point numerical pain rating scale. Pain. 2001;94(2):149-158. PMID 11690728.
    What the study says. Across 2,724 patients in 10 clinical trials, a drop of about 2 points, or about 30%, on the 0–10 scale corresponds on average to an improvement the patient judged important. A larger drop is needed when baseline pain is high. In OUCH. The report states whether the change in pain reaches this 30% threshold. The same benchmark (2 points or 30%) is used to detect flares, as a rise: this is an extrapolation, established for drops. It is a group average: it does not say what matters to the person.
  3. Haute Autorité de santé. Douleur chronique : reconnaître le syndrome douloureux chronique, l’évaluer et orienter le patient. Recommandations professionnelles, décembre 2008 (in French).
    What the study says. French guideline that defines chronic pain syndrome (persistent or recurrent pain with functional impact) and describes how to recognise it, assess it and refer the patient. In OUCH. French reference cited in the report for the assessment of chronic pain.
  4. Dworkin RH, Turk DC, Wyrwich KW, et al. Interpreting the clinical importance of treatment outcomes in chronic pain clinical trials: IMMPACT recommendations. J Pain. 2008;9(2):105-121. PMID 18055266.
    What the study says. Consensus of 40 participants (universities, agencies, patients, industry) on interpreting results: at least two methods are advised, and provisional benchmarks (about 30% drop: moderate; about 50%: substantial) are proposed. In OUCH. The report describes a 30% drop as moderately relevant and 50% as substantial. These benchmarks are designed for groups of patients, not for an individual.
  5. Boonstra AM, Stewart RE, Köke AJA, et al. Cut-off points for mild, moderate, and severe pain on the numeric rating scale for pain in patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain. Front Psychol. 2016;7:1466. PMID 27746750.
    What the study says. Across 2,854 chronic pain patients, the cut-offs depend on the impact on daily life: on average, mild ≤ 5, moderate 6–7, severe ≥ 8; among those who catastrophise little, mild ≤ 3, moderate 4–6, severe ≥ 7. In OUCH. OUCH uses mild ≤ 3, moderate 4–6, severe ≥ 7, conventional thresholds that match this second case. They are benchmarks, not a diagnosis.
  6. Broderick JE, Schwartz JE, Vikingstad G, et al. The accuracy of pain and fatigue items across different reporting periods. Pain. 2008;139(1):146-157. PMID 18455312.
    What the study says. In 83 patients followed with an electronic diary, recalled pain is inflated compared with ratings made through the day. Recall of the previous day remains faithful, but memory degrades beyond a few days. In OUCH. OUCH has the person rate the day just ended, day by day, rather than summarise the week from memory.
  7. Dixon WG, Beukenhorst AL, Yimer BB, et al. How the weather affects the pain of citizen scientists using a smartphone app. NPJ Digit Med. 2019;2:105. PMID 31667359.
    What the study says. “Cloudy with a Chance of Pain”: 2,658 patients rated their pain every day for 15 months. Links exist with humidity, atmospheric pressure and wind, but they are modest. In OUCH. OUCH records the day’s weather so the person can see whether it matters for them, without presenting the link as established.
  8. Salaffi F, Stancati A, Silvestri CA, Ciapetti A, Grassi W. Minimal clinically important changes in chronic musculoskeletal pain intensity measured on a numerical rating scale. Eur J Pain. 2004;8(4):283-291. PMID 15207508.
    What the study says. In 825 patients, a drop of one point (15%) is the smallest perceptible difference; a drop of 2 points (33%) corresponds to “much better”. A larger drop is needed when baseline pain is high. In OUCH. Confirms the 2-point or 30% benchmark used by the report.
  9. Diener HC, Kropp P, Dresler T, Evers S, Förderreuther S, Gaul C, Holle-Lee D, May A, Niederberger U, Moll S, Schankin C, Lampl C. Management of medication overuse (MO) and medication overuse headache (MOH) S1 guideline. Neurol Res Pract. 2022;4(1):37. PMID 36031642.
    What the study says. A guideline from the German, Austrian and Swiss neurology societies. It recalls the definition of medication overuse headache in the international classification of headache disorders: headache on at least 15 days a month in someone who already has a headache disorder, and regular use of an attack medication for more than 3 months, on at least 15 days a month for simple analgesics and at least 10 days a month for combination analgesics, triptans, ergot derivatives and opioids. In OUCH. For someone who notes days with an attack medication, the report restates these reference points in one sentence, with their source, next to the count. OUCH does not know the class of the medication (it is a box ticked), computes nothing and does not conclude: only a doctor can say a person is concerned. The classification itself could not be read at its source; these are the reference points as this guideline reports them.

  1. Ostelo RWJG, Deyo RA, Stratford P, et al. Interpreting change scores for pain and functional status in low back pain: towards international consensus regarding minimal important change. Spine. 2008;33(1):90-94. PMID 18165753.
    What the study says. Expert consensus for low back pain: a 2-point change on the 0–10 scale is a minimal important change, and a 30% improvement is a useful threshold whatever the scale. In OUCH. Confirms, by another route, the 2-point or 30% benchmark.

  3. Beukenhorst AL, Schultz DM, McBeth J, Sergeant JC, Dixon WG. Are weather conditions associated with chronic musculoskeletal pain? Review of results and methodologies. Pain. 2020;161(4):668-683. PMID 32195783.
    What the study says. Review of 43 studies: two in three find a link between weather and pain, but in varying directions, often in small groups followed for less than a month.   4. Smedslund G, Hagen KB. Does rain really cause pain? A systematic review of the associations between weather factors and severity of pain in people with rheumatoid arthritis. Eur J Pain. 2011;15(1):5-10. PMID 20570193.
    What the study says. Review of 9 studies in rheumatoid arthritis: on average, the link between pain and temperature, humidity or pressure is close to zero, but fewer than one patient in four reacts to it individually. In OUCH. Reaction to weather varies from person to person: OUCH looks at the person’s own data rather than the average.
  5. Steffens D, Maher CG, Li Q, et al. Effect of weather on back pain: results from a case-crossover study. Arthritis Care Res. 2014;66(12):1867-1872. PMID 25044376.
    What the study says. In 993 patients, temperature, humidity, pressure and rain do not increase the risk of acute low back pain. Strong wind increases it slightly, without clinical importance. In OUCH. Another reason to present the weather–pain link only as a lead to discuss.
  6. Finan PH, Goodin BR, Smith MT. The association of sleep and pain: an update and a path forward. J Pain. 2013;14(12):1539-1552. PMID 24290442.
    What the study says. Review: sleep disturbances predict the onset and worsening of chronic pain, and predict pain more strongly than pain predicts sleep. The link nonetheless runs in both directions. In OUCH. The report pairs pain with the previous night’s sleep, noting that the link runs both ways.
  7. Andrews NE, Strong J, Meredith PJ. Activity pacing, avoidance, endurance, and associations with patient functioning in chronic pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Arch Phys Med Rehabil. 2012;93(11):2109-2121. PMID 22728699.
    What the study says. Review of 41 studies: avoiding activity and pushing through to severe pain are both associated with more pain and disability. The direction of causation is not established. In OUCH. The report compares pain the day after a very active day, noting that too much and too little activity are both associated with more pain.
  8. Wolfe F, Clauw DJ, Fitzcharles MA, Goldenberg DL, Katz RS, Mease P, Russell AS, Russell IJ, Winfield JB, Yunus MB. The American College of Rheumatology preliminary diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia and measurement of symptom severity. Arthritis Care Res. 2010;62(5):600-610. PMID 20461783.
    What the study says. Preliminary American College of Rheumatology criteria for fibromyalgia, which no longer require a tender-point examination. The areas of “Where does it hurt?” follow the 19 WPI regions, with the left or right side, plus head, hands and feet. OUCH does not compute the WPI, which covers the past week and is for the doctor: it counts, day by day, what the person reports.
  9. Wolfe F, Clauw DJ, Fitzcharles MA, Goldenberg DL, Häuser W, Katz RL, Mease PJ, Russell AS, Russell IJ, Walitt B. 2016 Revisions to the 2010/2011 fibromyalgia diagnostic criteria. Semin Arthritis Rheum. 2016;46(3):319-329. PMID 27916278.
    What the study says. A revision of those criteria after a review of 14 validation studies. The WPI and its 19 regions are kept, and generalized pain requires pain in at least 4 of the 5 main body regions (jaw, chest and abdomen excluded). In OUCH. The same split of the body. The report says how many of those regions are reported over the period, as a plain count. OUCH applies neither these criteria nor the definition of generalized pain: only a doctor makes a diagnosis.

    What the study says. Thirteen expert rheumatologists examined 20 people with back pain.
